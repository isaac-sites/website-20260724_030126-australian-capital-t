## Introduction

In June 1983, an apparent aerial intrusion triggered one of the most unusual air defence responses in [New South Wales]({{ 'which-new-south-wales-ufo-cases-still/' | relative_url }})' UFO history. Radar operators tracking unidentified high-speed targets north of Sydney reported returns that appeared to be moving at extraordinary speeds, prompting RAAF Base Williamtown to place Mirage fighters on alert to investigate. At first glance, the incident looked like a classic military UFO case involving instrumented radar [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) rather than eyewitness testimony alone.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/New_South_Wales_f92f26_military_aviation_re_d61f55_williamtown_radar_il_3b0544-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Williamtown Alert illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/New_South_Wales_f92f26_military_aviation_re_d61f55_williamtown_radar_il_3b0544-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/New_South_Wales_f92f26_military_aviation_re_d61f55_williamtown_radar_il_3b0544-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The official investigation reached a very different conclusion. After examining the radar systems in detail, specialists determined that the mysterious tracks were not aircraft, unknown craft or hostile intruders. They were caused by a radar-processing artefact known as **"running rabbits"**—false targets created within the surveillance radar system itself. The Williamtown alert has since become one of Australia's clearest examples of how convincing radar anomalies can produce genuine military responses without any physical object being present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufoscans.com/a9755-8-3533443/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufoscans.com">[UFOScans.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufoscans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Operation Close Encounter — File 17/26/AIR &#124; UFOScans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 1, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 1983</span></span></span>

## How the radar tracks triggered a fighter response

The incident began in late June 1983 when the Department of Aviation's Sydney Route Surveillance Radar detected numerous unidentified contacts over the sea north of Sydney. Operators reported targets appearing at ranges of up to roughly 150 nautical miles and exhibiting behaviour unlike normal civil or military traffic.

What made the situation alarming was not simply the presence of unidentified returns but their apparent performance. Some tracks appeared to travel at estimated speeds ranging from hundreds to several thousand knots, occasionally changing direction or following spiral-like paths. Because these observations came from operational air traffic radar rather than visual sightings, controllers treated them as potential air-defence matters rather than curiosities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufoscans.com/a9755-8-3533443/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufoscans.com">[UFOScans.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufoscans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Operation Close Encounter — File 17/26/AIR &#124; UFOScans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 1, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 1983</span></span></span>

RAAF Base Williamtown was tasked with providing interceptor aircraft capable of identifying the unknown contacts. Operational orders were issued under the title **Exercise Close Encounter**, directing fighter units and supporting radar personnel to investigate the reported targets. The possibility that the returns represented unidentified man-made objects could not initially be ruled out, so a genuine military response was considered appropriate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/australia/A9755_8_3533443.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault AR 158</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Revised Oct 80 DEPAJ;TMENT OF DEFENC Par...</span></span></span>

The decision illustrates an important point in aviation safety. Military organisations routinely respond to uncertain radar information because failing to investigate a genuine unidentified aircraft would carry greater risks than launching an unnecessary interception.

## What "running rabbits" are and how they mimic targets

The investigation eventually shifted attention away from the sky and towards the radar equipment itself.

Engineers from the RAAF's No. 3 Control and Reporting Unit compared what controllers saw on their operational displays with the raw radar video generated before computer processing. This comparison proved decisive. The unexplained targets appeared on the processed operator displays but were absent from the underlying radar signal. That indicated the apparent aircraft were being created inside the radar processing system rather than reflecting real objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufoscans.com/a9755-8-3533443/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufoscans.com">[UFOScans.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufoscans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Operation Close Encounter — File 17/26/AIR &#124; UFOScans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 1, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 1983</span></span></span>

The phenomenon was identified as **"running rabbits"**, a term used by radar specialists for false moving targets produced by electronic interference or processing effects.

In practical terms:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the radar receives legitimate returns from terrain, weather or electronic noise;
* processing software attempts to convert these returns into moving tracks;
* under certain conditions, the processing creates artificial targets that appear to move rapidly across the display;
* operators naturally interpret these tracks as aircraft because they resemble genuine radar plots.

</div>

Unlike random screen noise, running rabbits can seem surprisingly coherent. They may maintain apparent headings, exhibit rapid acceleration or produce sequences of track positions that look convincing enough to prompt operational concern. Their apparent speed comes from the way the computer associates successive false returns rather than from any object actually travelling through the air.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufoscans.com/a9755-8-3533443/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufoscans.com">[UFOScans.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufoscans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Operation Close Encounter — File 17/26/AIR &#124; UFOScans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 1, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 1983</span></span></span>

## Why the technical explanation became convincing

Several features distinguish the Williamtown case from unresolved radar reports.

Most importantly, investigators were able to reproduce the discrepancy between processed displays and raw radar data. If genuine aircraft had been present, corresponding echoes would have appeared in the primary radar returns. Instead, the anomalous tracks disappeared when analysts examined the unprocessed information.

The investigation therefore identified a specific failure mechanism rather than merely proposing a possible explanation. Official findings concluded that the unidentified objects reported by Sydney air traffic control had been generated entirely by radar interference affecting the Route Surveillance Radar processing system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufoscans.com/a9755-8-3533443/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufoscans.com">[UFOScans.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufoscans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Operation Close Encounter — File 17/26/AIR &#124; UFOScans.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">June 1, 1983...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 1983</span></span></span>

This differs from many historical UFO cases where competing explanations remain plausible but unproven. At Williamtown, the evidence increasingly pointed towards an internal technical artefact rather than an unidentified aircraft whose identity was never established.

## Why the case matters in New South Wales UFO history

The Williamtown alert occupies an important place in New South Wales UFO history because it demonstrates that military involvement alone does not guarantee an unexplained event.

To many people, radar evidence appears inherently objective. The Williamtown investigation showed that radar is an instrument requiring interpretation. Complex surveillance systems include antennas, signal processors, software filters and operator displays, each capable of introducing misleading effects under particular circumstances.

The incident also highlights why defence organisations investigate unusual radar reports seriously. Personnel did not dismiss the contacts because they seemed improbable. Instead, they followed established procedures by treating the tracks as potential unknown aircraft until technical analysis demonstrated otherwise. That cautious approach strengthened, rather than weakened, confidence in the eventual explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://documents.theblackvault.com/documents/ufos/australia/A9755_8_3533443.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: documents.theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">documents.theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault AR 158</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Revised Oct 80 DEPAJ;TMENT OF DEFENC Par...</span></span></span>

Within the broader history of military and aviation UFO reports in New South Wales, Williamtown provides a useful counterpoint to cases such as the 1954 [Sea Fury]({{ 'sea-fury/' | relative_url }}) encounter near Nowra. Whereas the Sea Fury incident remains debated because no definitive mechanism explains every aspect of the report, the Williamtown alert ultimately illustrates how sophisticated radar artefacts can produce convincing but entirely illusory aerial targets.

## What the Williamtown incident teaches about radar UFO claims

The 1983 Williamtown alert remains one of Australia's strongest examples of a technically resolved military UFO incident.

Its lasting significance lies less in the initial alarm than in the investigation that followed. Rather than relying on speculation, investigators examined operational procedures, compared different forms of radar data and identified a specific electronic mechanism capable of generating the mysterious tracks.

For anyone assessing later UFO reports involving radar, the case serves as an important reminder that an unidentified radar contact is not automatically evidence of an unknown aircraft. Radar can reveal real objects beyond human sight, but it can also create convincing illusions.     Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }

        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',

      });

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;

      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });

    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();

        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);

        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
