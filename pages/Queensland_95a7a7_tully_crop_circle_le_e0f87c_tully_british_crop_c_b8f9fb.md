## Introduction

The 1966 Tully “[saucer nest]({{ 'saucer-nest/' | relative_url }})” incident in [Queensland]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-queenslands-ufo/' | relative_url }}) did not create the entire crop-circle phenomenon, but it played a surprisingly important role in its modern history. More than a decade after banana farmer George Pedley reported seeing a disc-like object rise from Horseshoe Lagoon and discovering a circular area of flattened reeds, two English men—Doug Bower and Dave Chorley—adapted the basic idea into a series of deliberate hoaxes in southern England. Their work transformed a simple Australian wetland mystery into the famous crop circles that became a global media sensation.

This connection matters because it separates two distinct historical questions. The first is whether anything unusual happened at Tully in 1966. The second is how that widely reported story inspired later people to manufacture similar-looking traces. Bower and Chorley's confession weakened claims that every mysterious circle indicated a UFO, but it did not prove that the original Queensland incident itself had been staged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-11-24/sugar-cane-farm-ufo-mystery-expanse-podcast-series-uncropped/104559256" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News Tully&#x27;s cane farm crop circles and an enduring 58yo UFO mystery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsTully&#x27;s cane farm crop circles and an enduring 58yo UFO mystery - ABC NewsNovember 24, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 24, 2024</span></span></span>

## What Bower and Chorley Borrowed from Australia

Doug Bower later explained that the idea came from reports he had read about the Australian “saucer nests” while living in Australia during the 1960s. Those reports described circular areas of flattened vegetation allegedly associated with UFO sightings, particularly the widely publicised Tully case. After returning to England, Bower suggested to his friend Dave Chorley that they recreate the appearance of a flying-saucer landing in an English field simply to see how people would react.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/crop-circles-the-art-of-the-hoax-2524283/?page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span></span></span>

What they borrowed was remarkably modest:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the concept of a circular landing trace rather than an elaborate symbol;
* the association between a physical ground mark and a reported flying saucer;
* confidence that newspapers would find such discoveries newsworthy because Tully had already demonstrated the public appeal of the idea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/crop-circles-the-art-of-the-hoax-2524283/?page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span></span></span>

</div>

This is an important distinction. Bower and Chorley did not claim that Australia already had geometric crop circles. Their inspiration came from the much simpler image of a circular landing mark left behind by an unidentified object.

## How the Wetland Nest Became a Field-Made Hoax

The original Tully formation differed greatly from the circles that later appeared across southern England.

The Queensland case involved flattened reeds in a lagoon. The British hoaxes instead appeared in standing cereal crops such as wheat and barley. Bower and Chorley realised that cereal fields offered several practical advantages. The patterns were visible from surrounding roads and hills, easier to photograph from the air and more dramatic than disturbed reeds floating on water.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/crop-circles-the-art-of-the-hoax-2524283/?page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span></span></span>

Using little more than wooden planks, rope and careful planning, they produced flattened circles that resembled what many people imagined a UFO landing site should look like. They worked mainly at night and relied on the assumption that observers would not expect such large formations to be created so quickly with simple tools. When challenged after their [1991 confession]({{ '1991-confession/' | relative_url }}), they demonstrated their methods publicly, successfully reproducing convincing circles before journalists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/crop-circles-the-art-of-the-hoax-2524283/?page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span></span></span>

Their demonstrations exposed an important weakness in earlier claims about crop circles: impressive appearance alone was not reliable evidence of an extraordinary cause.

## Why England Transformed the Idea into Geometric Art

The first circles made by Bower and Chorley were deliberately simple, closely reflecting the Australian saucer-nest concept. Over time, however, the phenomenon changed dramatically.

Several factors encouraged this evolution:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Growing media attention.** Newspapers increasingly rewarded larger and more visually striking formations.
* **Competition among circle makers.** Other groups began producing their own designs, often trying to surpass previous creations.
* **Changing public expectations.** Once simple circles became familiar, increasingly complex geometrical patterns attracted greater interest and publicity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/crop-circles-the-art-of-the-hoax-2524283/?page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span></span></span>

</div>

As a result, the English crop-circle phenomenon gradually became something quite different from the Queensland story that had inspired it. The focus shifted away from apparent landing marks towards intricate geometric artwork, mathematical motifs and increasingly elaborate pictographs.

Ironically, the success of the hoax encouraged many believers to argue that only some formations were human-made while others remained unexplained. Rather than ending debate, Bower and Chorley's confession divided crop circles into competing categories of suspected hoaxes and purported genuine mysteries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/crop-circles-the-art-of-the-hoax-2524283/?page=1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian Magazine Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax</span></span></span>

## What This Means for the Tully Case

One of the most common misunderstandings is that the British confession somehow debunked the original Queensland incident. Historically, that is not what happened.

Bower and Chorley admitted that Australian reports inspired their English creations. They did not claim responsibility for the 1966 Tully formation, nor did they suggest George Pedley had fabricated his account. Their confession demonstrated that convincing circular traces could be manufactured by people, making later crop-circle claims far less persuasive as evidence for UFOs.
