## Introduction

[Western Australia]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-western/' | relative_url }})’s police UFO file reveals something more solid than proof of extraordinary spacecraft: it preserves decades of sincere, near-contemporary witness testimony and shows how government officials handled reports they could not readily identify. Beginning with reports from 1951, the file brings together statements from members of the public, government employees and police officers across Perth, farming districts, mining communities and remote settlements. Police commonly recorded whether witnesses seemed sober, reliable, frightened or unlikely to invent a story, then passed stronger reports to the Royal Australian Air Force.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://slwa.wa.gov.au/stories/slwa-abc-radio/truth-not-out-there" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slwa.wa.gov.au">[State Library of Western Australia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slwa.wa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wa.gov.au.</span></span></span>

That process gives the archive genuine historical value, but it also exposes its limits. Judging that a person was honest did not establish the distance, size or speed of an unfamiliar light. Many files lack photographs, radar returns, instrument readings or complete follow-up forms. The records therefore support a careful conclusion: Western Australians repeatedly reported unusual things in the sky, and police often considered those reports worthy of documentation, but the archive cannot by itself show that the sightings had one cause or involved technology beyond known aircraft and natural phenomena.

## How the police file was created

The surviving collection was compiled by the Western Australian Police Department from reports received between 1951 and the mid-1970s. It contains dozens of accounts, with much of the activity concentrated in the 1950s and 1960s and many reports coming from country locations such as Bridgetown, Dalwallinu, Kojonup and Onslow. The file remained restricted for decades and became publicly accessible after a review of police records in 2007. It is now held in the State Records Office of Western Australia’s archival collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/listen/programs/perth-focus/ufo/12499372" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ABC News&#x27;UFO file&#x27; sheds light on strange encounters in Western Australia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC listen...</span></span></span>

The archive was not created as a self-contained scientific research project. It grew through ordinary administrative practice. A witness approached a local station, an officer took a statement or prepared a report, and material considered potentially significant could be forwarded through police headquarters to the RAAF. During the Cold War, the air force had practical reasons to collect unusual-aerial-sighting reports: unidentified observations might involve aircraft, rockets, satellites, re-entering space hardware or other matters relevant to defence and air safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-12-07/accessing-australia-secret-ufo-files/104673082" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsHow Bill Chalker became one of the first civilians to access the RAAF&#x27;s UFO files - ABC News...</span></span></span>

### The 1951 Bridgetown report

One of the earliest documented examples came from Charles Aubrey Bolton, a Lands Department inspector camping east of Bridgetown in 1951. Bolton described an apparently wingless aircraft-like object with orange and green lights and illuminated openings. He said it travelled from east to west at roughly jet speed, climbed sharply and disappeared as its lights seemed to go out. Bridgetown police sent a report to the police commissioner, and a copy reached RAAF Pearce.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thewest.com.au/news/wa/was-ufo-history-revealed-state-records-show-the-skies-over-wa-have-seen-many-unidentified-flying-objects-c-8450517" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thewest.com.au">[The West Australian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thewest.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on com.au.</span></span></span>

The response is instructive because it shows official interest without demonstrating official confirmation. The RAAF’s western-area commander called the matter “of considerable interest” and asked police to supervise Bolton while he completed a detailed questionnaire. The questions covered time, direction, appearance, course changes and any visible trail—the kinds of details needed to compare a report with aircraft or astronomical activity. Yet the copy of the form preserved in the state file has blank answer spaces. An apparently promising investigation therefore survives in an incomplete form, sharply limiting what can now be concluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thewest.com.au/news/wa/was-ufo-history-revealed-state-records-show-the-skies-over-wa-have-seen-many-unidentified-flying-objects-c-8450517" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thewest.com.au">[The West Australian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thewest.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on com.au.</span></span></span>

This is a recurring feature of historical UFO archives. A document may prove that an incident was reported and that officials responded, while still preserving too little information to determine what caused it. The existence of a government file establishes an administrative fact, not the physical nature of the reported object.

## What officers recorded about witnesses

Police reports frequently assessed the person as well as the sighting. Officers used descriptions such as “reliable”, “sensible”, “a person of standing” or “teetotaller”. Some noted that a witness had previously been sceptical about UFOs, while others recorded visible fear or distress. These remarks suggest that police were trying to decide whether reports arose from fabrication, intoxication or obvious instability before forwarding them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://slwa.wa.gov.au/stories/slwa-abc-radio/truth-not-out-there" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slwa.wa.gov.au">[State Library of Western Australia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slwa.wa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wa.gov.au.</span></span></span>

Such assessments matter because they weaken the simplest dismissive theory that every report was a joke or drunken tale. They do not, however, make the observations technically accurate. An honest and sober person can misidentify Venus, a meteor, an aircraft seen head-on, a balloon, distant lightning or reflected light. Human beings are especially poor at estimating the distance and speed of an isolated light when there is no visible horizon or known object for scale.

The language in the files also reflects the social standards of its era. Police often relied on occupation, reputation and perceived community standing as shorthand for credibility. That may help historians understand why one report advanced through official channels while another did not, but it is not a substitute for independent corroboration. A farm worker, government inspector or respected resident could give a sincere account without having the observational information needed for a reliable identification.

### Cunderdin’s “sensible” witnesses

In early 1954, police responded to RAAF inquiries about reports from Norseman and Cunderdin. Cunderdin officers said three residents were emphatic that the object they had observed was unlike an ordinary aircraft and described them as adult, sensible people of standing in the community. The wording strongly endorsed the witnesses’ honesty, but the surviving summary does not provide enough measured data to establish the object’s altitude, range or actual motion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thewest.com.au/news/wa/was-ufo-history-revealed-state-records-show-the-skies-over-wa-have-seen-many-unidentified-flying-objects-c-8450517" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thewest.com.au">[The West Australian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thewest.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on com.au.</span></span></span>

A separate Cunderdin report from April 1954 concerned Vera May Fulwood, whose children drew her attention to a round, flat, silver object. She described it as moving slowly and apparently rotating or rocking in a bird-like manner, without audible engine noise. The reporting constable, who had known her for about a year, said he saw no reason to treat her information as unreliable. Her report was passed to the RAAF.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thewest.com.au/news/wa/was-ufo-history-revealed-state-records-show-the-skies-over-wa-have-seen-many-unidentified-flying-objects-c-8450517" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thewest.com.au">[The West Australian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thewest.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on com.au.</span></span></span>

Fulwood’s statement is valuable because it preserves a named witness, location, description and police credibility assessment close to the date of observation. Its weakness is equally clear: there was no photograph, known distance, angular measurement or second independent observation described in the surviving account. “Silent” is not decisive when distance is uncertain, and apparent rotation can result from changing illumination, atmospheric distortion or an observer’s shifting perspective.

## What the statements actually describe

Across the file, witnesses repeatedly reported bright lights, metallic-looking forms and round or cylindrical objects. Some described pale green illumination, silence, abrupt direction changes or rapid disappearance. A 1966 statement from a farm worker near Kununurra, for example, became one of the illustrated records used by the State Library to demonstrate the character of the collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://slwa.wa.gov.au/stories/slwa-abc-radio/truth-not-out-there" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slwa.wa.gov.au">[State Library of Western Australia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slwa.wa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wa.gov.au.</span></span></span>

These recurring descriptions are intriguing, but similarity of language does not necessarily indicate one recurring machine. Terms such as “disc”, “cylinder” and “metallic” were part of the established flying-saucer vocabulary by the 1950s. Bright astronomical objects, [aircraft lights]({{ 'aircraft-lights-d03506/' | relative_url }}), meteors and illuminated clouds can also generate broadly similar descriptions from observers who lack a clear view of structure.

The geographical spread is more informative than any single repeated shape. Reports came through local police stations across an enormous state, rather than from one controlled observation site. This helped preserve experiences from people who might never have contacted a specialist UFO organisation, but it also produced uneven evidence. Different officers asked different questions; weather and aircraft checks were not always documented; and regional witnesses often observed objects against dark, open horizons where scale was particularly difficult to judge.

## Police records and RAAF investigation

The WA Police file sits within a wider national reporting system. For much of the post-war period, the RAAF was the main Australian body receiving and evaluating unusual aerial sightings. Reports could arrive from civilians, police, defence personnel and other government employees. The air force’s interest was tied chiefly to aerospace awareness and national security rather than an institutional belief that witnesses were seeing alien vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-12-07/accessing-australia-secret-ufo-files/104673082" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsHow Bill Chalker became one of the first civilians to access the RAAF&#x27;s UFO files - ABC News...</span></span></span>

Western Australian police therefore often acted as collectors and intermediaries. They could interview witnesses, record local circumstances and make preliminary checks, but specialised evaluation was normally passed upwards. This division of responsibility explains why a state police file contains correspondence with RAAF Pearce and reporting forms designed around aviation questions.

It also explains why the state archive should not be mistaken for a completed catalogue of official findings. Some answers or enclosures may have been retained in federal defence files rather than the police folder. Other reports appear to have been filed with little or no written resolution. National Archives records show that the RAAF accumulated thousands of reports over the decades, while officials also regarded much of the work as an increasing burden with little demonstrated defence value. The air force eventually ceased routinely collecting public UFO reports in the 1990s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-12-07/accessing-australia-secret-ufo-files/104673082" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: abc.net.au">[ABC News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">abc.net.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ABC NewsHow Bill Chalker became one of the first civilians to access the RAAF&#x27;s UFO files - ABC News...</span></span></span>

## Where official witnesses strengthen a case

A police officer, government inspector or aviation worker can add value to a report when professional experience bears directly on the observation. An air-traffic controller may recognise normal aircraft movement; a pilot may be familiar with navigation lights; an officer may document a witness’s condition immediately after an event. Official status also increases the likelihood that a dated written record will survive.

The strongest reports in any such archive would combine several features:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* independent witnesses observing from different positions;
* precise times, bearings and elevation angles;
* aircraft, weather and astronomical checks;
* photographs or film with reliable provenance;
* radar or other instrumental confirmation;
* consistent statements recorded before witnesses discussed the event together.

</div>

Most entries in the WA Police file meet only some of these criteria. They may contain credible testimony and careful police notes but lack the measurement and corroboration required to reconstruct an object’s path. Modern scientific proposals for investigating unidentified aerial phenomena emphasise synchronised cameras, triangulation, radar-derived range, acoustic sensors and environmental measurements precisely because eyewitness descriptions alone cannot reliably establish size, distance or speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

A witness saying that an object accelerated at an “astonishing” rate may be accurately reporting an impression. Without range information, however, angular movement across the sky cannot be converted into true velocity. A nearby slow object and a distant fast object can produce a similar visual effect.

## Why some reports stayed unexplained

An unresolved entry is not necessarily the strongest entry. In many cases, it simply means there was not enough information to test ordinary possibilities. Reports made hours or days later might lack an exact time. A witness might not know the direction faced. Police could find that no local aircraft was reported but remain unable to exclude a distant aircraft, meteor, astronomical body or atmospheric effect.

The 1953 Dalwallinu report illustrates the problem. Four residents reportedly saw an object in the western or south-western sky. Air traffic control and RAAF Pearce were said to have no aircraft in the area, while a government astronomer reportedly considered the described motion too fast for a meteorite. Those checks make the sighting more interesting than an unsupported anecdote, yet the surviving account still lacks the positional and duration data needed for a modern reassessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://thewest.com.au/news/wa/was-ufo-history-revealed-state-records-show-the-skies-over-wa-have-seen-many-unidentified-flying-objects-c-8450517" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thewest.com.au">[The West Australian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thewest.com.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on com.au.</span></span></span>

The absence of an identified aircraft is useful negative evidence, but it does not identify what was present. Flight records may be incomplete; an object’s apparent direction may be wrong; and a brief light can have many causes. Similarly, an astronomer’s opinion based on a witness description is not equivalent to a calculation from measured trajectory data.

Police correspondence rarely offered elaborate explanations. The State Library notes that many entries were passed on without attempts to explain them, leaving much of the mystery intact. That restraint can be read as open-mindedness, but it also reflects the limited role and resources of local police. Their principal task was to record what had been reported, not to conduct prolonged astronomical or aerospace analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://slwa.wa.gov.au/stories/slwa-abc-radio/truth-not-out-there" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slwa.wa.gov.au">[State Library of Western Australia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slwa.wa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wa.gov.au.</span></span></span>

## What the archive can prove

The file provides strong evidence for several limited but important conclusions. Western Australians were reporting strange aerial observations to police by 1951, before artificial satellites became a common potential explanation. Police did not automatically ridicule those witnesses. Officers recorded statements, assessed apparent credibility and sometimes forwarded cases to defence authorities. The archive also shows that UFO reporting extended well beyond Perth into agricultural, coastal, mining and remote communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://slwa.wa.gov.au/stories/slwa-abc-radio/truth-not-out-there" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: slwa.wa.gov.au">[State Library of Western Australia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">slwa.wa.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wa.gov.au.</span></span></span>

It can also document how individuals reacted. Statements about fear, agitation or a previous disbelief in UFOs tell historians that some experiences were personally powerful. They help reconstruct the social history of unexplained sightings and the relationship between country residents, police stations and federal authorities.

What the file cannot establish is that every witness saw the same class of object, that unexplained reports were immune to ordinary explanations, or that officials verified extraterrestrial technology. Police endorsements concerned the apparent sincerity of witnesses, not the aerodynamic performance or origin of what they described.

Nor does the file justify treating every missing answer or unresolved case as evidence of suppression. The incomplete Bridgetown questionnaire, fragmented correspondence and absence of final determinations are consistent with routine recordkeeping failures, the division of material between agencies and the low priority eventually assigned to cases with no clear defence significance.

## The most useful reading of the files

The WA Police UFO archive is best understood as a witness dataset shaped by government procedure. It is more dependable than folklore because it contains dated records and near-contemporary statements. It is less conclusive than a scientific dataset because observations were not made with standardised instruments, controlled methods or consistent follow-up.

Its most revealing feature is the distinction between **credible witness** and **verified interpretation**. Police could reasonably decide that someone was truthful, sober and genuinely frightened. That finding supports the reality of the person’s experience. It does not prove that the witness correctly judged an object to be enormous, close, silent or moving at extraordinary speed.

Within Western Australia’s wider UFO history, the file therefore matters less as a hidden store of confirmed craft than as a record of official uncertainty. It shows police taking unusual reports seriously enough to preserve them, defence authorities asking structured questions, and both institutions repeatedly confronting the same obstacle: a vivid description is not the same as measurable evidence. Some cases remain genuinely unresolved, but unresolved is the beginning of an assessment—not a conclusion about what was in the sky.
