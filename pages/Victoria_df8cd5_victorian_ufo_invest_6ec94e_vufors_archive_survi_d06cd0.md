## Introduction

Much of [Victoria]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-victorias-ufo/' | relative_url }})'s historical UFO record survives not because of a government archive, but because volunteers decided it was worth preserving. From the late 1950s onwards, the Victorian Flying Saucer Research Society (later the Victorian UFO Research Society, or VUFORS) collected witness statements, investigator notes, newspaper cuttings, correspondence and locally published reports that would otherwise have been discarded. These files have become an important source for historians reconstructing Victoria's UFO history because many official investigations were brief, selective or never publicly archived. At the same time, VUFORS' collections reflect the priorities and assumptions of a civilian research group rather than a neutral public record, meaning each document must be assessed on its own merits rather than treated as definitive evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_ufology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian ufology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 23, 2006 — In July 1952, Edgar Jarrold founded the Australian Flying Saucer Bureau (AFSB) in · Such was the interest in UFOs duri...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 23, 2006</span></span></span>

## From flying saucer society to VUFORS

The organisation that became VUFORS emerged during the rapid growth of Australian civilian UFO research in the 1950s. A Victorian branch of the Australian Flying Saucer Research Society was established at Melbourne University on 17 February 1957 under Melbourne lawyer Peter E. Norris. Later that year it became an independent organisation, the Victorian Flying Saucer Research Society (VFSRS), before adopting the name Victorian UFO Research Society (VUFORS) in 1968 as the term "UFO" replaced "flying saucer" in common use.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_ufology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian ufology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 23, 2006 — In July 1952, Edgar Jarrold founded the Australian Flying Saucer Bureau (AFSB) in · Such was the interest in UFOs duri...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 23, 2006</span></span></span>

The change in name reflected more than fashion. It marked an attempt to present the organisation as an investigative society rather than a club devoted to a single interpretation of unexplained aerial phenomena. Society publications repeatedly described UFO reports as a subject deserving careful examination rather than automatic belief or dismissal, even though many members personally considered that some cases might represent genuinely unexplained phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/Australian_UFO_Bulletin_1987_12_December/Australian_UFO_Bulletin_1987_12_December_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian UFO Bulletin 1987 12 December djvu.txt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">history of the Victorian U.F.O. Research Society. The earliest U.F.O. group to form in Australia was the “Australian Flying Saucer Bureau...</span></span></span>

Unlike government agencies, whose interest generally centred on aviation safety or defence implications, VUFORS regarded almost every report as historically valuable. Even sightings judged to have ordinary [explanations]({{ 'explanations/' | relative_url }}) could contribute to understanding patterns of public reporting, local media coverage and witness behaviour. This philosophy greatly increased the amount of material that survived.

## What the surviving archive contains

VUFORS preserved far more than simple sighting lists. A typical case file could accumulate documents over months or even years as investigators corresponded with witnesses, compared reports and revisited earlier conclusions.

The surviving collections commonly include:

* original witness letters and completed report forms;
* handwritten interview notes and follow-up correspondence;
* witness sketches showing object shape, movement or location;
* investigator diagrams and maps;
* newspaper clippings from metropolitan and regional Victorian newspapers;
* internal newsletters and bulletins discussing recent reports;
* correspondence exchanged with other Australian and overseas research groups;
* occasional letters to or from government departments, airports or defence authorities where inquiries were made.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/Australian_UFO_Bulletin_1987_12_December/Australian_UFO_Bulletin_1987_12_December_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian UFO Bulletin 1987 12 December djvu.txt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">history of the Victorian U.F.O. These materials often preserve details absent from later books or documentaries. Early witness descriptions were recorded before memories changed, media narratives developed or famous cases acquired decades of folklore. Even where investigators reached conclusions now considered doubtful, the original documentation remains valuable because it records what witnesses actually reported at the time.

The society also produced its own publications, including the *Australian Flying Saucer Review* and later the *Australian UFO Bulletin*. Besides reporting new cases, these publications documented organisational history, reproduced historical newspaper material and preserved summaries of investigations that might otherwise have disappeared completely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/Australian_UFO_Bulletin_1987_12_December/Australian_UFO_Bulletin_1987_12_December_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian UFO Bulletin 1987 12 December djvu.txt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">history of the Victorian U.F.O. ## Why volunteer records became indispensable

Victoria illustrates a broader problem in UFO history: official documentation is uneven.

The Royal Australian Air Force and other government bodies investigated selected reports, particularly where national security or aviation safety might have been relevant. Many routine civilian sightings, however, received little or no official attention, while some records were never retained or were later dispersed through archival processes. Volunteer investigators therefore became the only people systematically preserving reports that fell outside official priorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_ufology" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian ufology</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">January 23, 2006 — In July 1952, Edgar Jarrold founded the Australian Flying Saucer Bureau (AFSB) in · Such was the interest in UFOs duri...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 23, 2006</span></span></span>

The 1966 [Westall]({{ 'westall/' | relative_url }}) sighting demonstrates why this mattered. Although the incident later became Australia's best-known mass UFO sighting, much of the surviving detail comes from interviews, recollections, newspaper reports and later civilian research rather than a comprehensive official investigative file. Civilian collections preserved witness names, sketches, press coverage and personal accounts that continued to inform later historical work as new witnesses came forward decades afterwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://blogs.slv.vic.gov.au/our-stories/strange-lights-in-the-sky-the-westall-ufo-event-1966/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: blogs.slv.vic.gov.au">[State Library Victoria Blogs]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">blogs.slv.vic.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">strange lights in the sky the westall ufo event 1966</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">State Library Victoria BlogsStrange lights in the sky: The Westall UFO event, 19666 Apr 2024 — On 6 April 1966 something strange happened...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 1966</span></span></span>

VUFORS also undertook historical reconstruction projects. Rather than collecting only contemporary sightings, members assembled earlier Victorian reports from newspaper archives, creating compilations that recovered cases dating back before the organisation itself existed. ## Preservation did not eliminate bias

The value of the archive should not be confused with proof that unusual objects were observed.

Volunteer investigators decided which reports deserved attention, what questions to ask and which supporting material to retain. Some files are extensive because investigators found them intriguing, while equally genuine reports may have received only brief treatment. Missing files do not necessarily indicate insignificant events, and detailed files do not necessarily indicate stronger evidence.

The organisation also evolved over decades, with changing committee members, investigative styles and editorial priorities. Peter Norris, Judith Magee, Paul Norman and other long-serving members all influenced how reports were documented and interpreted at different times. Another limitation is survival itself. Volunteer archives are inherently fragile. Papers can be misplaced during committee changes, private collections may remain in family hands, and some material has been lost through relocation, deterioration or simple lack of resources. Consequently, today's archive represents only a surviving portion of what VUFORS once collected.

## Why the archive still matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

For anyone studying Victoria's UFO history, VUFORS' greatest achievement was preserving evidence that would probably have vanished. The archive captures how witnesses described unusual aerial sightings before later retellings reshaped the stories, documents how civilian investigators approached unexplained reports across several decades, and preserves local newspaper coverage that often exists nowhere else in assembled form.

</div>

Its significance therefore lies less in proving extraordinary claims than in documenting an important aspect of Victoria's social and cultural history. The files show what people reported, how investigators responded, how public understanding changed over time and where gaps remain. Used alongside official records, contemporary newspapers and later scholarly analysis, the surviving VUFORS archive provides one of the richest surviving windows into Victoria's disappearing UFO record.
