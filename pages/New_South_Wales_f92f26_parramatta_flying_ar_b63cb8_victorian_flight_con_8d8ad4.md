## Introduction

Frederick William Birmingham's 1868 vision of a "[flying ark]({{ 'flying-ark/' | relative_url }})" is easiest to understand when placed alongside the flight culture of Victorian [New South Wales]({{ 'which-new-south-wales-ufo-cases-still/' | relative_url }}) rather than the later language of UFOs. By the time Birmingham described an enclosed aerial vessel carrying him into a pilot's compartment, colonial Australians had already spent decades reading about balloons, experimental flying machines and ambitious airship proposals. Newspapers, public exhibitions and engineering discussions had made controlled flight seem an attainable—if still unrealised—technological goal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/stories/dreamers-experimenters-and-balloonists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sl.nsw.gov.au">[State Library of New South Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sl.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsw.gov.au.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/New_South_Wales_f92f26_parramatta_flying_ar_b63cb8_victorian_flight_con_8d8ad4-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Flight Culture illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/New_South_Wales_f92f26_parramatta_flying_ar_b63cb8_victorian_flight_con_8d8ad4-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/New_South_Wales_f92f26_parramatta_flying_ar_b63cb8_victorian_flight_con_8d8ad4-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This context matters because Birmingham did not describe an impossible magical object. Instead, he imagined what looked like an engineered vehicle designed to solve a recognised nineteenth-century problem: how human beings might travel safely through the air before the invention of the aeroplane. His account therefore reflects a distinctive blend of Victorian engineering optimism, religious symbolism and speculative aviation, rather than [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) that colonial New South Wales experienced a modern-style UFO encounter.

## Ballooning made flight seem imaginable

### Colonial audiences already expected humans to fly

Long before powered aircraft existed, people in New South Wales had become familiar with the idea of aerial travel through newspaper reports and public demonstrations. International developments were widely reprinted in colonial newspapers, including descriptions of balloon ascents in Europe and proposals for mechanically powered flying machines.

One influential example arrived in 1843, when Sydney readers learned about William Samuel Henson's proposed Aerial Steam Carriage, an ambitious design for a steam-powered aircraft. Although the machine never flew, it introduced colonial readers to the possibility that engineering—not myth—might eventually conquer the air.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/stories/dreamers-experimenters-and-balloonists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sl.nsw.gov.au">[State Library of New South Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sl.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsw.gov.au.</span></span></span>

Public ballooning reinforced that message. Balloon ascents became major attractions because they showed that people could genuinely leave the ground, even if they had little control over where they landed. Successes and spectacular failures alike demonstrated that flight belonged to the world of experimental science rather than pure fantasy.

### Sydney's balloon experiments attracted enormous attention

Before Birmingham's 1868 vision, Sydney had already witnessed several highly publicised balloon attempts:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* French aeronaut Pierre Maigre's failed ascent in the Domain in 1856 drew thousands of spectators before ending in disorder when the balloon would not fly.
* William Dean successfully ascended in the gas balloon *The Australasian* in 1858, proving that sustained human flight in Australia was possible, even without powered navigation.
* Later balloon exhibitions throughout the 1860s and 1870s continued to attract large crowds despite frequent accidents and dramatic mishaps.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/stories/dreamers-experimenters-and-balloonists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sl.nsw.gov.au">[nsw.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sl.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsw.gov.au.</span></span></span>

</div>

These events created a public culture in which aerial vehicles were exciting, experimental and imperfect. Flight itself was no longer unimaginable; the remaining challenge was controlling it.

## Australian flying-machine schemes before 1868

The most important local influence was not a balloon but an Australian-designed airship.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://airshipsonline.com/airships/australian-airship-scheme/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airshipsonline.com">[airshipsonline.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airshipsonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Australian Airship Scheme</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Introduction 2. Sydney physician and inventor Dr William Bland spent years developing his "Atmotic Ship", first publicised in 1851. His design combined a hydrogen-filled elongated balloon with steam-powered propellers intended to provide steering and propulsion rather than simply drifting with the wind. Models and detailed drawings were displayed internationally at London's Crystal Palace and in Paris, demonstrating that colonial New South Wales was contributing ideas to global debates about aviation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/stories/dreamers-experimenters-and-balloonists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sl.nsw.gov.au">[nsw.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sl.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsw.gov.au.</span></span></span>

Although Bland never built a full-sized machine, the proposal was widely discussed. More importantly, it framed aerial navigation as an engineering problem awaiting a practical solution rather than an impossible dream.

For someone like Birmingham—an engineer and surveyor accustomed to technical drawing and mechanical thinking—that distinction mattered. His imagination was already operating within a society where inventors openly proposed navigable aerial craft decades before powered flight became reality.

Some researchers have even noted that Bland died in 1868, the same year as Birmingham's vision, although there is no evidence that this coincidence directly influenced Birmingham's experience. Bill Chalker has suggested only that the wider atmosphere of contemporary aeronautical experimentation may have helped shape how Birmingham interpreted his vision, not that Bland's design served as its blueprint.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theozfiles.blogspot.com/2013/11/did-frederick-william-birmingham-build.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theozfiles.blogspot.com">[The Oz Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theozfiles.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">did frederick william birmingham build</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Oz Filestheozfiles: Did Frederick William Birmingham build a flying machine based on his bizarre 1868 UFO &quot;vision&quot; in Parramatta, NSW...</span></span></span>

## Why an enclosed aerial vessel seemed plausible

One striking feature of Birmingham's account is that the "ark" was described as an enclosed machine with substantial walls and an internal pilot's area rather than an open platform or balloon basket.

From a modern perspective this resembles later aircraft interiors, but within the Victorian context it reflected contemporary engineering expectations. Designers increasingly imagined future flying machines as vehicles containing engines, steering systems and protected working spaces. The concept of a pilot operating machinery inside a purpose-built structure already existed in speculative aviation literature, even though no practical aircraft yet embodied it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/stories/dreamers-experimenters-and-balloonists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sl.nsw.gov.au">[State Library of New South Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sl.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsw.gov.au.</span></span></span>

Equally significant is what Birmingham did **not** describe. He did not present a seamless metallic disc or an inexplicable object beyond human technology. Instead, he believed he had been shown engineering principles that could eventually be understood and reproduced.

That emphasis places his experience much closer to Victorian invention than to twentieth-century UFO narratives.

## Technology and revelation were not opposites

Modern readers often separate scientific invention from religious experience, but many Victorians did not.

Birmingham interpreted the figure accompanying him as a spirit rather than an extraterrestrial visitor. The aerial machine itself became part of a larger revelation in which technical knowledge and divine purpose could coexist. Receiving practical information through a supernatural vision was therefore consistent with wider nineteenth-century religious culture, particularly among people who believed scientific discovery revealed God's design rather than challenged it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au/blog/2014/01/09/frederick-william-birmingham" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au">[Parramatta History and Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsw.gov.au.</span></span></span>

This helps explain why Birmingham reportedly attempted to develop or sketch a flying machine afterwards. He appears to have believed he had received genuine engineering insight through an extraordinary spiritual experience, not that he had merely witnessed an unexplained object.

## What this means for New South Wales UFO history

Understanding Victorian flight culture changes how Birmingham's account fits into the history of unusual aerial experiences in New South Wales.

Rather than standing as an isolated anticipation of modern flying-saucer reports, the Parramatta vision occupies a transitional space between three nineteenth-century traditions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* public fascination with balloons and experimental aviation;
* colonial confidence that engineering would eventually master flight;
* religious visions interpreted through contemporary technological ideas.

</div>

This does not prove that Birmingham fabricated his experience, nor does it confirm that he encountered an unknown craft. Instead, it shows why his description naturally adopted the language of the most advanced flight concepts available in 1868. His "flying ark" reflected a society already imagining controlled aerial navigation decades before aeroplanes became reality, making the vision historically distinctive while also firmly rooted in the technological imagination of Victorian New South Wales.

  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
