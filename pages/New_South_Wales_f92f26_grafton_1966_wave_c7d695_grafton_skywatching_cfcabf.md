## Introduction

The increase in UFO reports around Grafton during 1966 did not necessarily mean that more extraordinary objects appeared over northern [New South Wales]({{ 'which-new-south-wales-ufo-cases-still/' | relative_url }}). It also reflected the fact that many more people were actively looking at the sky after local sightings received widespread publicity. Once residents expected something unusual, ordinary lights that would previously have gone unnoticed were examined, discussed and, in some cases, reported as possible UFOs. This pattern is well recognised in both historical UFO investigations and research into human perception: increased observation creates more genuine reports, but it also increases the number of sincere mistakes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap03.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 3: Psychological Aspects of UFO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 3: Psychological Aspects of UFO Reports</span></span></span>

## What inexperienced observers commonly misperceive

Most people spend relatively little time studying the night sky. When a community suddenly begins organised or informal skywatching, many observers encounter bright celestial or aviation objects without having a reliable frame of reference.

During the [Grafton wave]({{ 'grafton-wave/' | relative_url }}), residents who had heard about police sightings or newspaper reports were more likely to notice lights that previously would have been ignored. Common sources of confusion include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Bright planets, particularly when low on the horizon, where atmospheric turbulence makes them appear to shimmer, change colour or pulse.
* Bright stars that seem unusually prominent under clear winter skies.
* Aircraft approaching or departing at a distance, whose landing lights can appear stationary before suddenly changing direction as the aircraft alters its heading.
* Meteors, satellites and other brief astronomical events that can surprise observers unfamiliar with their normal appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufouap.net/en/explainers/common-ufo-misidentifications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufouap.net">[ufouap.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufouap.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufouap.net.</span></span></span>

</div>

None of these explanations automatically accounts for every report from Grafton. However, investigators of UFO waves have consistently found that periods of heightened public attention generate many sincere reports that are ultimately traced to ordinary celestial or aviation phenomena.

## How expectation altered descriptions of movement

One of the most important features of the Grafton episode was not simply that people were looking upwards, but that they were looking with the expectation of seeing something extraordinary.

Psychological research has long shown that expectation influences perception, particularly when people observe small points of light against a dark background with few visual reference points. A stationary light can appear to drift, accelerate or zigzag because of tiny involuntary eye movements, an illusion known as the autokinetic effect. The effect becomes especially convincing when observers are already searching for unusual aerial activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap03.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 3: Psychological Aspects of UFO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 3: Psychological Aspects of UFO Reports</span></span></span>

Expectation also affects interpretation. Two people may watch the same distant light, yet one describes an aircraft while another, already familiar with reports of mysterious objects over Grafton, interprets identical movements as intelligent manoeuvres. Memory can reinforce this process after the event, with later discussions and media coverage influencing how witnesses recall speed, direction or unusual behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-astrobiology/article/psychological-aspects-in-unidentified-anomalous-phenomena-uap-witnesses/7D23058E10309384DDAA8E15607485CC" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University PressPsychological aspects in unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP) witnesses &#124; International Journal of Astrobiolo...</span></span></span>

This helps explain why later descriptions within a sighting wave often become more dramatic even when independent physical [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) does not increase.

## Why public attention multiplied reports

Grafton's 1966 wave illustrates a feedback loop that has appeared in many historical UFO flaps.

A widely reported sighting encourages more people to watch the sky. More observers naturally notice more unusual-looking lights. Increased discussion leads more people to report what they have seen. Those additional reports then reinforce the public belief that something exceptional is happening, encouraging still more observation.

Importantly, this cycle can develop without any deliberate exaggeration. Honest witnesses may report exactly what they believe they observed, while investigators later conclude that different reports referred to unrelated objects or ordinary phenomena seen under unfamiliar circumstances. The Condon Report, one of the most influential scientific reviews of UFO reports, devoted considerable attention to these well-established perceptual mechanisms rather than assuming deception by witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap03.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 3: Psychological Aspects of UFO Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Condon Report, Sec VI, Chap 3: Psychological Aspects of UFO Reports</span></span></span>

Within the Grafton context, this makes the growing number of reports understandable without requiring every sighting to represent the same object or event.

## Why dramatic reports outlast mundane identifications

Another reason Grafton's wave appears more mysterious in retrospect is that unusual stories tend to survive better than ordinary explanations.

A newspaper headline about police pursuing an unidentified light is memorable. A later suggestion that the observation may have involved an aircraft, a bright planet or another conventional source rarely receives equivalent attention. As a result, historical accounts often preserve the dramatic report while the subsequent investigation receives much less public notice.

This imbalance affects many UFO cases. Researchers who revisit old reports frequently discover that some sightings were plausibly explained, while others remain too poorly documented for firm conclusions. The unresolved cases naturally attract the greatest interest, whereas mundane identifications gradually disappear from popular memory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufouap.net/en/explainers/common-ufo-misidentifications" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufouap.net">[ufouap.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufouap.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufouap.net.</span></span></span>

For Grafton, surviving evidence supports a cautious interpretation. The publicity surrounding the 1966 sightings almost certainly encouraged many genuine observations, but it also increased the likelihood that ordinary celestial and aviation objects would be interpreted as extraordinary.

## What this means for understanding the Grafton wave

The increase in false alarms should not be viewed as evidence that the entire Grafton sighting wave was imaginary, nor as proof that every report had an ordinary explanation. Instead, it demonstrates why investigators must evaluate each account individually.

The historical significance of Grafton lies partly in showing how a local period of intense public interest transformed skywatching itself. More people looked upward, more people noticed unfamiliar lights, and more people reported them.
