## Introduction

Frederick William Birmingham’s “flying ark” is often promoted as one of Australia’s earliest UFO encounters. In July 1868, the Parramatta engineer said he experienced a vision of an airborne machine, entered it with a human-shaped “spirit”, and received information that might reveal how such a craft worked. The story is remarkable because it appeared decades before aeroplanes and nearly eighty years before the modern flying-saucer era.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/New_South_Wales_f92f26_parramatta_flying_ar_b63cb8-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Flying Ark" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Yet the surviving account does not read like a conventional report of an unknown aircraft. Birmingham repeatedly called the experience a **vision**, interpreted its figure as a spirit and believed the machine’s secret had been disclosed through divine favour. He recorded the episode retrospectively in a memorandum book associated with 1873, not in a surviving independent report made on the night. The most defensible interpretation is therefore that this was a Victorian religious and technological revelation, rather than firm [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) that an exotic craft landed in Parramatta Park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au/blog/2014/01/09/frederick-william-birmingham" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au">[nsw.gov.au]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">frederick william birmingham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Parramatta History and HeritageFrederick William Birmingham31 Dec 2023 — In July 1868, Birmingham had his first encounter with a &quot;UFO vis...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1868</span></span></span>

## What Birmingham Said He Saw

Frederick William Birmingham was not an anonymous storyteller. Born in Ireland in 1821, he trained in surveying and engineering before settling in [New South Wales]({{ 'which-new-south-wales-ufo-cases-still/' | relative_url }}). He worked as Parramatta’s town surveyor or council engineer, served as an alderman and participated in local civic affairs. His professional background helps explain why later UFO writers found his testimony attractive: he appeared to be a technically minded witness describing a machine before successful powered flight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au/blog/2014/01/09/frederick-william-birmingham" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au">[Parramatta History and Heritage]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">historyandheritage.cityofparramatta.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-title">frederick william birmingham</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Parramatta History and HeritageFrederick William Birmingham31 Dec 2023 — In July 1868, Birmingham had his first encounter with a &quot;UFO vis...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 1868</span></span></span>

The principal narrative survives through a document titled as a copy from Birmingham’s memorandum book, concerning “a machine to go through the air”. Birmingham placed the central episode on the night of 25 July 1868. He described being at his Parramatta home when he became aware of strange presences or communications, followed by an experience involving Parramatta Park.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/kbcat/kbmoreintoz.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: Keith Basterfield - A CATALOGUE OF THE MORE INTERESTING AUSTRALIAN UAP REPORTS - Updated June 3, 2016June 3, 20...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 3, 2016</span></span></span>

In the account, Birmingham encountered an ark-like aerial structure and a figure with the form of a man. The being was not presented as a visitor from another planet. Birmingham described it in spirit-like terms, with a muted or shadowy appearance and ordinary human dress. The figure guided him into the machine and towards what Birmingham called its pilot house. There he believed he was shown a formula or principle connected with aerial navigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theozfiles.blogspot.com/2024/08/the-birmingham-ufo-vision-manuscript.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theozfiles.blogspot.com">[theozfiles.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theozfiles.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the birmingham ufo vision manuscript</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the birmingham ufo vision manuscript</span></span></span>

Some physical details sound strikingly technological when isolated from the wider narrative. Birmingham referred to thick sides, considerable strength and an enclosed structure designed to travel through the air. He later drew, designed or attempted to develop an aerial machine inspired by what he believed had been revealed. These details have encouraged comparisons with modern craft interiors and close-encounter reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theozfiles.blogspot.com/2018/07/the-old-and-new.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theozfiles.blogspot.com">[theozfiles.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theozfiles.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">theozfiles: The Old and the New</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">theozfiles: The Old and the New</span></span></span>

However, the episode also included features that do not fit a straightforward observation of a landed vehicle:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Birmingham treated the event as a personal revelation rather than a public aviation incident.
* The guide was understood as a spirit, not a biological pilot or space traveller.
* Religious language, prophecy and divine purpose were central to his interpretation.
* No independent witness is known to have confirmed an ark descending into Parramatta Park.
* The detailed surviving narrative was written or compiled several years after the alleged event.

</div>

Those points do not establish that Birmingham was dishonest. They do mean that his text cannot safely be reduced to “an engineer saw a spacecraft”.

## Religion, Invention and Personal Revelation

Birmingham’s experience makes better sense when placed within the imaginative world of the 1860s. Victorian society did not draw a clean boundary between religion, science, invention and the supernatural. Spiritualism had become a visible subculture, with séances, spirit communications, automatic writing, specialist publications and attempts to investigate unusual phenomena systematically. Some believers even argued that spiritual manifestations could be studied through observation and scientific law.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://victorianweb.org/religion/spirit.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: victorianweb.org">[victorianweb.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">victorianweb.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on victorianweb.org.</span></span></span>

This does not prove that Birmingham belonged to an organised spiritualist group. It does show that a technically educated Victorian could discuss spirits, messages and machinery without experiencing the categories as contradictory. In his narrative, the ark was both an engineered object and an instrument of revelation. The machine’s design was supposedly communicated through a supernatural encounter, placing Birmingham somewhere between visionary, inventor and religious seeker.

Flight was also a genuine technological preoccupation. Balloons had carried people since the eighteenth century, and public balloon demonstrations were familiar in colonial Australia. Sydney witnessed attempted and successful ascents before 1868: Pierre Maigre’s failed 1856 launch in the Domain provoked a riot among disappointed spectators, while William Dean’s gas balloon travelled about 30 kilometres in 1858.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sl.nsw.gov.au/stories/dreamers-experimenters-and-balloonists" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sl.nsw.gov.au">[State Library of New South Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sl.nsw.gov.au</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">State Library of New South WalesDreamers, experimenters and balloonistsThroughout the mid 19th century several balloon flights were made...</span></span></span>

Heavier-than-air flight remained unsolved, but it was not unimaginable. Nineteenth-century inventors produced concepts for gliders, powered craft, helicopters and aerial ships. Australian proposals included William Bland’s “atmotic ship”, an elaborate airship scheme publicised before Birmingham’s vision. Birmingham therefore did not need knowledge of modern UFOs to imagine an enclosed navigable vessel in the sky. He lived in an age when balloons were real, mechanical flight was an open engineering problem and speculative designs circulated alongside religious expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eaglepubs.erau.edu/introductiontoaerospaceflightvehicles/chapter/history-of-aircraft-and-aviation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eaglepubs.erau.edu">[erau.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eaglepubs.erau.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Eagle Pubs History of Aircraft &amp; Aviation –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eagle Pubs History of Aircraft &amp; Aviation –</span></span></span>

That setting also explains the ark imagery. An ark was not simply a shape. It carried biblical associations of preservation, divine instruction and a specially constructed vessel. Birmingham’s choice of language suggests that the object’s religious meaning was at least as important to him as its engineering.

## A Story Written After the Event

The evidential weakness of the case begins with its documentation. Birmingham’s main account is associated with a memorandum book dated 1873, roughly five years after the July 1868 experience. The surviving material has circulated through copies and private hands rather than through a clearly documented contemporary police report, council file or newspaper account published immediately after the alleged landing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/kbcat/kbpre47.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[project1947.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project 1947PROJECT 1947: Keith Basterfield - A CATALOGUE OF PRE 24 JUNE 1947 AUSTRALIAN UNIDENTIFIED AERIAL PHENOMENA - Version 7Citing...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: JUNE 1947</span></span></span>

That delay matters. Memory is not a recording, particularly when an experience is repeatedly reconsidered and fitted into a developing personal belief. By 1873, Birmingham appears to have regarded the 1868 vision as the beginning of an unfolding mission to discover aerial navigation. His later writing may therefore preserve not only what he originally perceived, but also years of interpretation.

Birmingham described another event dated 9 March 1873. After observing unusual clouds or an aerial form in daylight, he concluded that the earlier revelation was being progressively clarified and that the flying machine could be realised. This second episode strengthened the story for Birmingham, but it does not independently confirm the first one: it comes from the same witness and the same developing system of belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/forum/bcausenc.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on project1947.com.</span></span></span>

Later historical research has added evidence that Birmingham genuinely pursued flying-machine ideas. Reports indicate that he prepared designs, a model or some form of proposed craft, and attempted to obtain interest or financial support. One later newspaper reference described him returning to Parramatta after travelling to San Francisco to display a model of a flying machine. This supports the conclusion that his aeronautical ambition was sincere. It does not verify the supernatural origin he assigned to the design.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theozfiles.blogspot.com/2013/11/did-frederick-william-birmingham-build.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theozfiles.blogspot.com">[theozfiles.blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theozfiles.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">did frederick william birmingham build</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">did frederick william birmingham build</span></span></span>

The distinction is important: there is evidence for **Birmingham the visionary inventor**, but not independent evidence for **a physical ark landing in Parramatta Park**.

## Why Modern UFO Labels Can Mislead

The term “UFO” can be used broadly to mean anything seen in the sky that remains unidentified. Under that loose definition, Birmingham’s story can sit in a catalogue of unusual aerial experiences. Problems arise when the modern label silently changes the content of the original claim.

A twentieth-century spacecraft reading tends to translate the story as follows: the ark becomes an advanced vehicle, the spirit becomes a humanoid occupant, the revelation becomes technical instruction, and Birmingham’s visionary state becomes a close encounter. Each substitution sounds plausible only because readers already know the conventions of modern UFO narratives.

Birmingham himself supplied a different framework. He used spiritual and religious concepts, described visionary experiences and saw the machine as part of a providential disclosure. To remove those elements is not merely to update old-fashioned language. It is to replace the witness’s interpretation with one created much later.

There is also a chronology problem. The detailed memorandum’s existence before the flying-saucer era is genuinely interesting, but being early does not make it extraterrestrial. Victorian culture already contained airborne vessels, biblical vehicles, spirit guides, prophetic dreams and engineering speculation. Birmingham’s narrative combined ideas available in his own period rather than anticipating only the imagery of the late twentieth century.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.victorianvoices.net/topics/transportation/air.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: victorianvoices.net">[victorianvoices.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">victorianvoices.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on victorianvoices.net.</span></span></span>

Nor should Birmingham’s later personal difficulties be used as a simple dismissal. Secondary biographical accounts describe growing obsession, financial collapse, suspicious or paranoid thinking and eventual confinement in an asylum. Such reports are relevant to assessing the development of his beliefs, but retrospective mental-health labels cannot determine exactly what he experienced on one night in 1868. They weaken confidence in literal interpretations without proving deliberate fabrication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://backstorypress.com/tag/ufo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: backstorypress.com">[backstorypress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">backstorypress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on backstorypress.com.</span></span></span>

## What the Case Contributes to New South Wales UFO History

Parramatta’s flying ark matters because it demonstrates how easily historical experiences can be reshaped by later expectations. Unlike the aviation, radar and military cases that dominate twentieth-century New South Wales UFO history, Birmingham’s account emerged from a world in which visionary religion and technological invention could occupy the same story.

The case has several real historical strengths. Birmingham was identifiable, technically trained and deeply committed to the idea of aerial navigation. A substantial written narrative exists, and later evidence indicates that he pursued a flying-machine project rather than merely telling an isolated tale. His descriptions are therefore more significant than an unattributed legend repeated long after the supposed event.

Its weaknesses are equally substantial. The fullest account was retrospective; there is no known independent witness to the ark; no physical evidence survives from the alleged encounter; and the narrative openly identifies itself with vision, spirit communication and divine revelation. Later reporting has clarified Birmingham’s identity and inventive activities, but it has not supplied external confirmation that an unknown craft was physically present.

The fairest classification is therefore **historically important but evidentially weak as a UFO case**. It should not be presented as a debunked hoax, because there is little reason to think Birmingham was simply inventing a story for entertainment. Nor should it be treated as an unresolved aircraft encounter on the same footing as a report supported by pilots, radar records or multiple observers.

Birmingham appears to have had an intense experience that he considered real and life-changing. What caused it cannot now be established. A dream, waking vision, altered state, religious experience or other private perceptual event fits the surviving evidence more closely than the claim that a technologically advanced vehicle landed unnoticed in Parramatta Park.

## The Most Credible Reading

Parramatta’s flying ark was probably not a “UFO” in the modern popular sense of a mysterious physical craft, still less evidence of extraterrestrial visitation. It was a Victorian vision shaped by Frederick Birmingham’s engineering ambitions, religious ideas and fascination with the unsolved problem of human flight.

Its value lies precisely in that ambiguity. The account shows that people could imagine complex aerial machines and human-like supernatural operators long before flying saucers entered popular culture. But it also warns against assuming that every old story resembling a modern encounter belongs to the same phenomenon.

Within New South Wales UFO history, Birmingham’s ark is best understood as a cultural and biographical starting point: an unusually well-developed nineteenth-century account of perceived aerial technology, preserved through one man’s personal revelation. Later discoveries have strengthened the history of Birmingham and his proposed machine. They have not strengthened the case for a physical landing.
